# Reviewer Voice

## Canonical author-facing writing style

The author-facing review MUST follow the **exact linguistic style** of the original review reproduced in [Canonical Reviewer Style](canonical-reviewer-style.md), preserved verbatim in that reference. Read the complete source example before drafting or editing any review. Its opening assessment and four comments are the primary few-shot style reference, not merely examples of general politeness.

Imitate the reviewer's academic voice: sentence structure, phrasing and vocabulary, natural hedging, connective explanations, parenthetical qualifications, manuscript-specific detail, variation in paragraph length, and progression from observations to questions. The result should plausibly read as if written by that reviewer, without copying the example's substantive claims or mechanically repeating its exact phrases. Write correct English rather than reproducing accidental typos in the original.

Scientific scrutiny and style are separate. Verify observations with the manuscript and the evidence-driven review process first, then express the supported concerns in this voice. The chosen profile controls the strength of scrutiny and recommendation, **not** the author-facing writing style. The same voice applies to second-round reviews, subject to their existing brevity and outcome requirements.

## Opening assessment

Follow the opening paragraph's overall rhythm rather than using a checklist:

- Describe the particular problem addressed by the paper and, where warranted, its method and practical relevance.
- Acknowledge concrete strengths supported by the actual manuscript without inflated or formulaic praise.
- Introduce subsequent comments as questions or suggestions for clarification, when appropriate.

Write one continuous, natural paragraph, not an assessment table, labeled summary, or mechanical strengths-and-weaknesses formula. For a markedly flawed paper, preserve the measured voice without inventing positive judgments.

## Select concerns before drafting

Rank findings by their effect on the central contribution, correctness, reproducibility, and interpretation. The numbered comments should address the strongest distinct manuscript-specific concerns rather than reproduce a full internal audit. Four to six comments are often sufficient, but neither the count nor the length is a hard limit. Combine repeated concerns and group closely related questions into coherent comments. Consolidate minor textual or formatting issues where appropriate.

## How individual comments should read

The canonical source demonstrates several natural constructions, all of which are permitted:

- A specific observation about a section or figure followed by a question about an unclear method.
- An explanation of what is stated in the manuscript, followed by a tentative practical implication and one or more connected questions.
- A direct question about how measurements or samples were selected, optionally followed by a question about stability or sensitivity.
- A statement about contrasting results, followed by clarification of the criterion used to distinguish the compared conditions.

Begin with the manuscript, the experimental context, or an immediate scientific question, whichever best matches the issue. Develop the concern through connected sentences. Use explicit question marks for unresolved procedures, assumptions, classifications, sampling decisions, denominators, and implementation details. Allow multiple closely related questions in one comment, as the canonical source demonstrates. Do not impose a fixed context-observation-question-impact order, a fixed number of sentences, or a rule that every comment must finish with an impact statement.

The reviewer may use formulations such as `I could not determine`, `Is this the same ...?`, `How were ...?`, `it would appear`, or `A little discussion would be welcome` when they arise naturally. Vary them according to context rather than repeatedly pasting a stock phrase. Retain the original example's formal but unforced academic cadence.

Write ordinary paragraphs numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on. Do not add mini-headings, bold topic labels, severity annotations, issue/impact/action labels, or separate questions as checklist items. Preserve the scientific detail required to make a question answerable, without manufacturing equipment inventories or demanding every conceivable experiment.

## Match epistemic status to phrasing

### Unresolved method or assumption

State what the manuscript appears to say, and ask how the unresolved aspect was handled. Do not turn uncertainty into a confirmed failure. Additional explanatory questions may be included when they concern the same scientific point.

### Confirmed error or inconsistency

Identify the precise verified defect, its location, and the correction needed. Direct language is warranted by evidence. Maintain the source reviewer's measured syntax rather than softening a demonstrated error into an empty question.

### Claim broader than supporting evidence

Describe the scope of the demonstrated result and inquire about the asserted extension or suggest a narrower conclusion. Require new experiments only where indispensable to a central claim. Otherwise, invite clarification or acknowledge the need for discussion and limitation.

## Stylistic constraints

- Do not substitute generic professionalism for close imitation of the **canonical opening assessment** and comments.
- Avoid repetitive `The authors must`, `Please report`, `The paper lacks`, or `I recommend` openings.
- Do not adopt a prosecutorial, adversarial, or confrontational voice even when the selected profile is strict.
- Do not insert praise-criticism sandwiches, rhetorical attacks, or mandatory impact sentences.
- Avoid formulaic templates that make every comment the same length or syntactic shape.
- Use manuscript locations, details, and terminology, but never transfer facts from the canonical example to a different paper.
- Do not imitate original typographical errors.
- Follow the skill's output format, confidentiality, and scientific-evidence rules. Style must not override factual accuracy.

## Final style verification

Read only the opening assessment and numbered comments in sequence, with the canonical example nearby. Revise any text that:

- sounds like a checklist rather than the canonical reviewer
- could have been written without inspecting this manuscript
- states an uncertain premise as a verified fact
- substitutes demands for connected methodological questions
- enforces one question or identical structure in every paragraph
- requests new work when discussion or appropriately scoped conclusions would suffice
- omits or distorts a verified serious scientific concern for the sake of sounding pleasant

The primary stylistic acceptance criterion is resemblance to the canonical reviewer's prose, not merely politeness or the presence of question marks. This is a qualitative writing review, not a property that static contract tests alone can prove.
